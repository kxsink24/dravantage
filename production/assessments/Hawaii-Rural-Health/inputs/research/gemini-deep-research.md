# Executive Briefing: Hawaii Rural Health Transformation Program (RHTP) and Statewide Data Readiness Assessment

## Executive Summary

The State of Hawaii is currently navigating a highly complex, capital-intensive inflection point in its healthcare delivery and data governance architecture. Catalyzed by a monumental $188.9 million annual award from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) under the Rural Health Transformation Program (RHTP)—totaling nearly $1 billion over a five-year performance period (2026–2031)—the state is mandated to radically modernize its care delivery, digital infrastructure, and value-based payment readiness1. Because 95.1% of Hawaii's land area is classified as rural, the implications of this funding touch nearly every operational facet of the state's health ecosystem, spanning emergency medical services, telehealth expansion, workforce development, and electronic health record (EHR) interoperability1.

This transformation is deliberately intertwined with Hawaii's participation in the CMS AHEAD (All-Payer Health Equity Approaches and Development) model, which will introduce hospital global budgets and advanced primary care payment models beginning in 20285. The intersection of RHTP capital injection and impending AHEAD mandates has created an urgent, high-stakes environment for the Hawaii Department of Health (DOH), the State Health Planning and Development Agency (SHPDA), and the Department of Human Services (DHS) Med-QUEST Division. These agencies are tasked with rapidly deploying funds to stand up new clinical networks, establish statewide data-exchange hubs, and fortify independent providers against downside financial risk4.

However, an exhaustive analysis of the public record reveals deep, systemic vulnerabilities in the state's existing enterprise architecture and data governance capabilities. The state's ongoing, multi-year attempt to build an Integrated Data and Analytic Platform (IDAP) to serve as its All-Payer Claims Database (APCD) has been heavily encumbered by vendor misalignment, delayed data definitions, tooling conflicts, and severe resource constraints, threatening both go-live schedules and project budgets9. Simultaneously, the Hawaii Health Information Exchange (HHIE) continues to face provider adoption hurdles, with 64% of surveyed providers relying on manual data entry or scanning rather than fluid interoperable exchange10.

For an upcoming Data Readiness Assessment (DRA) under the codename Hawaii-Rural-Health, proposing a massive, top-down clinical data re-architecture will likely encounter intense institutional fatigue and political friction. The optimal, low-friction entry point lies in addressing the immediate operational panic generated by the RHTP funding itself: grants administration, compliance visibility, and provider network coordination (specifically Virtual CIN/MSO readiness). By targeting the administrative and operational silos where state agencies are actively seeking solutions to manage the influx of federal funds, technology partners can deliver rapid time-to-value while organically establishing the foundational data models required for broader population health analytics.

## Source-Quality and Confidence Notes

The findings, strategic insights, and operational footprints detailed in this report are synthesized exclusively from publicly available documents, encompassing legislative testimonies, state procurement portals, official meeting minutes, Independent Verification and Validation (IV&V) assessments, and federal grant databases. To guide strategic decision-making and pre-sales validation, evidence is categorized by confidence level based on source reliability and temporal proximity.

High-confidence public facts include statutory mandates governing state agencies, as outlined in Hawaii Revised Statutes (HRS) Chapter 323D, which dictates the administrative boundaries of SHPDA and the state's health planning parameters11. The financial structure of the RHTP—specifically the $188.8 million annual award and its division into six core initiatives—is confirmed via CMS award databases and official steering committee presentations3. Furthermore, the profound project management struggles, schedule delays, and vendor conflicts within the Med-QUEST Health Analytics Program (HAP) are validated by a continuous series of monthly IV&V reports submitted to the state legislature between 2022 and 2024, providing an unvarnished view of internal execution capabilities9.

Medium-confidence interpretations surround the exact operational boundaries for RHTP fund disbursement between SHPDA, DOH, and Med-QUEST. While SHPDA explicitly claims management of the $45 million Rural Health Information Network (RHIN) and the $25 million Rural Value-Based Innovation (RVBI) portfolios8, the precise procurement mechanisms for sub-grantees—such as Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) and mobile health providers—appear to remain in active, fluid development5. Similarly, the competitive positioning of specific vendors requires careful interpretation; for instance, Unite Us is documented as the incumbent for closed-loop social referrals at the state Workforce Development Council, but its guaranteed expansion into DOH's RHTP initiatives remains a hypothesis rather than a confirmed mandate15.

Low-confidence unknowns requiring direct customer validation include the incumbent software systems currently utilized by SHPDA and DOH for grant lifecycle management and RHTP compliance tracking. While Salesforce is heavily utilized and highly regarded in other Hawaii departments—most notably the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA) and the Department of Agriculture, driven by implementation partner Pacific Point17—its specific penetration into the core DOH grants management ecosystem remains an internal hypothesis. Validating whether DOH intends to leverage existing state enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, such as Infor, or procure a purpose-built grants management system will be a primary objective of the discovery phase.

## Organizational Identity and Mandate

Understanding the operational boundaries, statutory limitations, and inter-agency dynamics of Hawaii's health apparatus is critical, as RHTP funds will be distributed across a matrix of organizational silos, requiring intensive cross-departmental coordination.

### State Health Planning and Development Agency (SHPDA)

Administratively attached to the Department of Health, SHPDA functions as the lead agency for health system reform, capacity planning, and resource allocation. Under Hawaii Revised Statutes (HRS) Chapter 323D, SHPDA holds the statutory authority to administer the state's Certificate of Need (CON) program, dictating the expansion or reduction of healthcare facilities to prevent redundant capitalization11. The agency is led by Administrator Dr. Jack Lewin, a highly influential figure who dual-hats as the Senior Advisor to Governor Josh Green on Healthcare Innovation21.

Recent legislative activity has significantly modernized and expanded SHPDA's mandate. Through the 2026 passage of HB 2319, the statutory definition of healthcare was broadened to explicitly include oral health, behavioral health, and long-term care22. More importantly, the legislation redefined SHPDA's primary function to promote "universal access to high-quality, equitable, and affordable health care for ALL the people of the State," positioning the agency to force collaboration across historically siloed departments, including DOH, DHS, and the Hawaii Health Systems Corporation (HHSC)22. SHPDA is positioned as the primary architect and coordinator for the state's most strategic RHTP initiatives, specifically managing over $70 million annually to modernize health IT interoperability and establish value-based care networks ahead of the AHEAD model rollout8.

### Med-QUEST Division (MQD) and the Health Analytics Program (HAP)

Med-QUEST serves as Hawaii's Medicaid agency, operating under the Department of Human Services (DHS), and provides coverage for the state's most highly vulnerable populations. The operational and funding history of the state's All-Payer Claims Database (APCD) highlights a complex jurisdictional handoff between SHPDA and Med-QUEST. Initially established under SHPDA's authority via a 2013 federal grant and reinforced by Act 139 in 2016, the APCD struggled with funding continuity23. Following the passage of Act 55 in 2018, the statutory authority and operational funding for the APCD were transitioned to Med-QUEST's newly established Health Analytics Program (HAP) to leverage CMS matching funds, though SHPDA retains the legal authority to compel data submission from private insurers and the Hawaii Employer-Union Health Benefits Trust Fund (EUTF)23.

Led by Administrator Ranjani Starr, the Med-QUEST HAP is positioned as the primary data owner and steward of the state's most complex, resource-intensive claims data integration project—the Integrated Data and Analytic Platform (IDAP)—and shares overarching RHTP steering responsibilities with SHPDA and the DOH1.

### Healthcare Ecosystem Partners and Academic Institutions

The execution of Hawaii's health initiatives relies heavily on academic and community partnerships, reflecting the state's highly federated approach to program delivery. The University of Hawaii (UH) is deeply embedded in state data governance and workforce development. The UH Telecommunication and Social Informatics Program (TASI) and the John A. Burns School of Medicine (JABSOM) serve as key subawardees for the RHTP1. UH researchers are tasked with defining clinical flags and indicators for the APCD data models, drafting data submission guides, and executing the HOME RUN workforce pipeline initiative to recruit and retain rural healthcare professionals1.

The Hawaii Health Information Exchange (HHIE) operates as the state-designated non-profit entity responsible for clinical data exchange. Founded in 2006, HHIE acts as the technical and operational bridge between acute care hospitals, ambulatory practices, and state agencies, utilizing the Health eNet platform to facilitate real-time transitions of care27.

Community-Based Organizations (CBOs) represent the critical "last mile" of care delivery, executing the state's equity mandates for unhoused, justice-impacted, and geographically isolated populations. Entities such as Project Vision Hawaii operate mobile hygiene units, vision screening vans, and street medicine programs, making them prime candidates for RHTP sub-grants under initiatives aimed at physical access and medical respite14.

## Rural Health Transformation Program (RHTP) Briefing

The Rural Health Transformation Program represents an unprecedented federal investment in Hawaii's healthcare infrastructure, catalyzed by the national $50 billion RHTP pool31. On December 29, 2025, CMS awarded the State of Hawaii a cooperative agreement totaling $188,892,440 for the first fiscal year, scaling to nearly $1 billion over a five-year performance period ending in October 20303. Coordinated across a statewide effort managed by consulting firm McAllister & Quinn, the proposal is structured to address severe geography-induced access challenges and prepare the ecosystem for the 2028 rollout of the CMS AHEAD model2.

### Compelling Events, Timelines, and Funding Cycles

The temporal alignment of the RHTP funding cycle with the impending CMS AHEAD model launch creates a highly compelling event for an immediate Data Readiness Assessment. Year 1 of the RHTP (January 1, 2026 – September 30, 2026) is explicitly designated as a "Pre-Implementation/Planning Period"5. During this narrow window, SHPDA, DOH, and Med-QUEST must design request for proposal (RFP) parameters, procure vendor solutions, distribute sub-grants, and establish stringent compliance tracking mechanisms before the aggressive rollout of AHEAD global budgets in January 20285.

The pressure to disburse $70 million annually through the competitive RHIN and RVBI funds will force SHPDA to rapidly deploy a secure, auditable grants-management and provider-readiness tracking architecture7. Furthermore, the RVBI initiative relies heavily on establishing a Virtual Clinically Integrated Network (CIN) or Managed Services Organization (MSO) to administer readiness payments, provide practice transformation support, and manage downside risk for independent primary care providers6. The procurement and deployment of the CRM and analytics infrastructure to support this MSO represents an immediate, high-priority technology acquisition for the state.

## Healthcare Data and Interoperability Landscape

Hawaii’s health data ecosystem is characterized by a stark bifurcation between clinical data exchange, managed primarily by the non-profit HHIE, and claims-based population analytics, managed by the Med-QUEST HAP. Bridging this divide is a central objective of the RHTP, yet profound technical and cultural barriers remain.

### Clinical Data Exchange: Hawaii Health Information Exchange (HHIE)

HHIE utilizes the Health eNet platform, powered by InterSystems, to securely route transition-of-care documents, laboratory results, medications, and real-time electronic alerts across the state10. The exchange acts as a critical utility; during an average week, approximately 3,600 providers access records on over 26,000 patients10.

Despite over a decade of operation, HHIE continues to battle significant data quality constraints, primarily surrounding duplicate patient records and identity matching across disparate, siloed hospital and clinic EHRs. In early 2023, HHIE contracted with 4medica to deploy a cloud-based Health Data Quality Platform, aiming to achieve an unprecedented 1% patient record duplication rate through advanced identity management37.

Interoperability adoption at the provider level remains stubbornly low. A 2023 Provider Interoperability Survey conducted by HHIE and the Healthcare Association of Hawaii (HAH) revealed that 64% of respondents still rely on manual data entry or scanning, citing prohibitive vendor costs, lack of technical resources, and security concerns as primary barriers10. An attempt by the state legislature in 2023 (HB 517) to mandate real-time data exchange between providers and the DOH was deferred, indicating that punitive legislative mandates for interoperability lack political traction38. Consequently, HHIE’s 2024–2028 strategic plan pivots toward expanding analytics capabilities, supporting SDoH data initiatives, and evaluating the federal Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement (TEFCA) landscape through incentivized, voluntary participation39.

### Claims Data Exchange: The All-Payer Claims Database (APCD)

The state's ambition to create a unified view of healthcare costs and utilization through an APCD has spanned more than a decade40. While SHPDA retains the statutory authority to mandate data submissions from government employee plans and commercial insurers, the technical execution resides within the Med-QUEST HAP23.

The APCD relies on a multi-tiered data architecture known as the Integrated Data and Analytic Platform (IDAP). The technical stack heavily leverages Snowflake for scalable data warehousing and Virtual Desktop Infrastructure (VDI) for secure, advanced analytics, running alongside Optum Symmetry for sophisticated risk scoring, episodic grouping, and total cost of care modeling9. The system is designed to ingest historical claims data dating back to 2009, capturing inpatient hospital, outpatient primary care, pharmacy, and specialty care services across Medicare, Medicaid, and private payers23.

### Closed-Loop Referrals and Community Integration

To address SDoH and health equity, various Hawaii organizations have begun adopting closed-loop referral platforms to connect clinical settings with community resources. The state Workforce Development Council and the Faith Collaborative currently utilize Unite Us for social care referrals15. However, the RHTP RHIN initiative specifically outlines funding for a new "Closed Loop Referral System and Care Coordination Hub" (C-Hub)5. This creates a competitive inflection point: the state must either dramatically expand the footprint of incumbent solutions like Unite Us or procure a competing platform capable of integrating seamlessly with both HHIE clinical feeds and Med-QUEST claims data.

## Governance, Privacy, and Accountability Map

The public record reveals severe, systemic friction regarding data governance, vendor management, and project execution. This friction poses the greatest single risk to Hawaii's health technology modernization efforts and must be meticulously navigated by any external technology partner.

### Statutory and Regulatory Governance

The foundational governance of state health data is dictated by HRS Chapter 323D-18.5 and operationalized through Hawaii Administrative Rules (HAR) Title 11 Chapter 18812. These statutes grant SHPDA regulatory authority over administrative data submissions, stipulating stringent privacy protections, including the mandatory stripping of direct personal identifiers (e.g., names, specific postal addresses) before data enters the APCD12. Furthermore, because the IDAP is fundamentally tied to Medicaid funding and infrastructure, the state must adhere to both HIPAA regulations and highly restrictive CMS MARS-E 2.2 security standards13.

### Execution Friction: The IDAP IV&V Findings

While the statutory framework is sound, the operational execution of data governance within the Med-QUEST HAP has been deeply troubled. A continuous series of Independent Verification and Validation (IV&V) reports, submitted to the state legislature by the Chief Information Officer between 2022 and 2024, paint a picture of a state struggling to manage a fragmented, multi-vendor ecosystem9.

The IDAP project relies on a complex matrix of contracted entities: a Project Management Office (PMO) vendor, a Data Governance vendor, a Design, Development, and Implementation (DDI) vendor, alongside academic researchers from the University of Hawaii13. IV&V reports repeatedly flagged the lack of clearly documented roles, leading to gaps in integration, decision-making bottlenecks, and siloed workstreams13.

Schedule and scope creep have been pervasive. By early 2024, the IDAP go-live date had been delayed to the end of the calendar year, primarily because University of Hawaii resources failed to deliver critical business rules for clinical "Flags and Indicators" on time, preventing the DDI vendor from completing dashboard designs and data modeling9. Compounding the issue, the state allowed the DDI vendor to bypass ServiceNow (the state's default ticketing system) in favor of Jira, while simultaneously utilizing Micro Focus ALM for requirements traceability and defect management. IV&V explicitly warned that this dual-system approach creates profound integration challenges, duplication of effort, and inflated project costs9. The financial implications of these delays are stark; by February 2024, the DDI vendor had consumed roughly $400,000 of an $8.8 million budget before an Integrated Master Schedule (IMS) was even baselined, and eleven change requests (CRs) were pending review, threatening the total project budget9.

The underlying trend is clear: the state acts capably as a policy authority (SHPDA) and a visionary funder (Med-QUEST), but struggles profoundly on an operational level as a data owner and system integrator. The failure to baseline an Integrated Master Schedule and the chronic delays in defining simple clinical data dictionaries suggest a foundational disconnect between the state's procurement mechanisms and agile software delivery paradigms. Any proposed Data Readiness Assessment must emphasize lightweight governance models, highly visible compliance tracking, and rapid, agile deployment, actively avoiding the bespoke, multi-vendor gridlock that has stalled the APCD.

## Strategic Priorities and Executive Outcomes

Executive leadership—spanning the Governor's Office, SHPDA, Med-QUEST, and the legislature—has consistently telegraphed the state's strategic priorities through public statements and grant abstracts. Technology solutions pitched during the DRA must be firmly anchored to these business outcomes rather than isolated technical capabilities.

### 1. AHEAD Model Readiness and Financial Viability

The impending transition to the CMS AHEAD model is the center of gravity for Hawaii's health policy. Dr. Jack Lewin has explicitly stated that the overarching goal of the RHTP RVBI fund is to help independent clinics and rural hospitals "manage financial risk and advanced participation"7. Technology solutions must directly enable practice transformation, downside risk modeling, total-cost-of-care analytics, and shared-savings distribution mechanisms2. The state recognizes that without sophisticated analytics and workflow support, rural providers will face financial insolvency under global budgets.

### 2. Administrative Burden Reduction

Provider burnout and administrative abrasion are acute pain points. A 2025 legislative mandate (Act 151) requires SHPDA to rigorously collect, analyze, and publish data on prior authorization claims, denials, and adjudications6. Solutions that can seamlessly ingest claims data, automate compliance reporting, and provide transparency into payer behavior will strongly align with SHPDA's mandate to streamline prior authorization workflows by 2026.

### 3. Workforce Pipeline and Rural Infrastructure Expansion

Governor Josh Green’s public statements heavily emphasize modernizing physical rural health infrastructure and expanding primary and behavioral health services on neighbor islands45. This strategic priority manifests in the HOME RUN workforce pipeline initiative (aimed at recruitment and retention) and the RICA initiative, which seeks to stabilize emergency transport and trauma transfers via a new, real-time MEDICOM coordination center2.

### 4. Health Equity and SDoH Integration

Treating highly vulnerable, complex populations—including Native Hawaiians, COFA (Compact of Free Association) citizens, and the unhoused—is a foundational priority for the DOH and Medicaid25. The RHTP Rural Respite Network aims to reduce preventable hospital readmissions for individuals experiencing homelessness by routing them to community care settings4. This effort is supported by localized, mobile interventions from CBOs like Project Vision Hawaii, who require lightweight case management systems to track health screenings, hygiene services (Hiehie mobile showers), and justice-impacted reentry support14.

## Technology and Operating-Model Footprint

Hawaii’s public sector utilizes a hybrid technology footprint, blending modern, cloud-native platforms with entrenched legacy systems. State IT strategy is heavily federated, with departments operating highly independent application stacks, often relying on local implementation partners for strategic capacity22.

### The Salesforce Footprint

Salesforce is highly entrenched across the State of Hawaii, functioning as the platform of choice for complex regulatory, licensing, and grants management modernization efforts. The Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA) successfully executed a massive legacy migration onto Salesforce for its Professional and Vocational Licensing (PVL) division18. Moving off 1980s-era technology, the implementation involved migrating 7 million legacy records, integrating 15 distinct interfaces, and supporting over 180 license types18. The business outcomes were profound: application processing times improved by 600%, and the setup time for activating newly legislated license types dropped from four weeks to just three hours18.

Beyond the DCCA, Salesforce implementations are documented at the Department of Agriculture, the Business Registration Division (BREG), and the State Laboratories Division, where it integrates via API with payment gateways for Merchant ID processing17. Crucially, the DOH recently procured an Electronic Disease Surveillance System (EDSS) built on the Salesforce platform, demonstrating existing DOH familiarity with the ecosystem51.

### Data, Analytics, and Integration

For heavy data warehousing and analytics, the state utilizes Snowflake for the APCD, paired with Optum Symmetry for clinical risk scoring, and Tableau for data visualization, the latter being actively promoted locally via state-sponsored TRUE Hawaii workshops9. Financial and ERP processes are managed through Infor, which is currently undergoing a statewide modernization effort (EFS)48. Integration patterns are fragmented; clinical routing relies on InterSystems (Health eNet) and 4medica, while social care routing defaults to Unite Us15.

## Procurement and Competitive Context

The vendor landscape in Hawaii is highly relationship-driven, with distinct incumbents operating across specific functional domains.

### Key Implementation Partners and Vendors

Pacific Point operates as a dominant, highly entrenched Salesforce implementation partner. Headquartered in Honolulu, with a deep portfolio of state contracts (including the highly successful DCCA and Dept of Agriculture implementations), they possess significant political and operational capital17. Pacific Point is highly active in local technology advocacy, sponsoring the Salesforce Hawaii Innovation Day, participating in the TRUE Hawaii initiative, and holding extensive public-sector procurement vehicles53. Their deep familiarity with state ERP integration and legacy data migration positions them as a formidable partner or competitor in any DOH/SHPDA technology acquisition.

For the APCD, Med-QUEST awarded multiple Indefinite Delivery/Indefinite Quantity (IDIQ) contracts for the IDAP under RFP-MQD-2021-015, resulting in the current, heavily siloed vendor ecosystem encompassing separate PMO, Data Governance, and DDI vendors13. Milliman is documented as the incumbent provider for actuarial services59. In the social care coordination space, Unite Us holds incumbent status with the Workforce Development Council, making them the natural competitor for any RHTP-funded Care Coordination Hub15.

### Funding Fluidity and Competitive Dynamics

The $189 million annual RHTP award operates as a cooperative agreement, injecting massive liquidity into the ecosystem. A substantial portion of this funding—specifically the $45 million RHIN and $25 million RVBI buckets—will be disbursed as competitive sub-awards to local hospitals, FQHCs, and independent clinics via Request for Proposals (RFPs) managed by SHPDA and DOH5. This structural dynamic means the state is simultaneously a buyer of enterprise administrative software (to manage the grant lifecycle) and a funder of decentralized clinical software (EHR upgrades, analytics hubs for sub-grantees).

## Candidate First-Use-Case Shortlist

To bypass the multi-vendor gridlock and clinical data definition battles that have stalled the APCD, the Data Readiness Assessment should focus on high-urgency, bounded use cases tied directly to the RHTP Year 1 pre-implementation phase.

Based strictly on public evidence, the following four candidate use cases are ranked by feasibility, speed to value, and alignment with documented executive pain signals:

### 1. RHTP Sub-Grant Lifecycle & Compliance Visibility (Highest Feasibility)

- [ ] **The Opportunity:** SHPDA and DOH face an immediate operational crisis: they must rapidly design RFPs, evaluate applications, and administer over $70M annually to rural hospitals and independent providers via the competitive RHIN and RVBI funds7. They require an auditable CRM system to track award disbursements, monitor sub-grantee milestone achievements, and generate 100% accurate CMS compliance reports to secure future funding tranches.
- [ ] **Business Sponsor:** Dr. Jack Lewin (SHPDA Administrator) / DOH Grant Administrators.
- [ ] **Data/Operational Owner:** SHPDA Operations / DOH Finance.
- [ ] **Required Data Sources:** State financial systems (Infor/EFS), applicant portal inputs, CMS reporting schemas.
- [ ] **Governance Risk:** Low. This use case relies entirely on administrative and financial data, successfully bypassing the friction of HIPAA compliance, clinical data-sharing agreements, and APCD data dictionaries.
- [ ] **Measurable Outcome:** Reduction in time-to-disbursement for rural sub-grantees; automated generation of CMS cooperative agreement compliance reporting.

### 2. RVBI Virtual CIN / MSO Provider Relationship Management

- [ ] **The Opportunity:** Under Initiative 6 (RVBI), SHPDA is establishing a Managed Services Organization (MSO) to help independent providers assume downside financial risk for the impending AHEAD model6. The state (or the MSO vendor) requires a robust Provider Relationship Management (PRM) system to track provider outreach, credentialing, onboarding status, and the delivery of practice transformation coaching.
- [ ] **Business Sponsor:** Dr. Jack Lewin (SHPDA) / RVBI Program Director.
- [ ] **Data/Operational Owner:** The competitively selected MSO Vendor / SHPDA.
- [ ] **Required Data Sources:** Provider credentialing registries, HHIE participation status, APCD risk scores (via Optum Symmetry integrations).
- [ ] **Governance Risk:** Medium. Requires basic provider demographic and operational metrics, but avoids patient-level clinical data ingestion in Phase 1.
- [ ] **Measurable Outcome:** Percentage of independent rural providers successfully contracted, coached, and fully onboarded to the AHEAD risk model prior to January 2028.

### 3. Community Care Coordination Hub (C-Hub)

- [ ] **The Opportunity:** Initiative 1 (RHIN) explicitly allocates funds for a "Closed Loop Referral System and Care Coordination Hub" (C-Hub)5. This aligns perfectly with the Rural Respite Network's mandate to safely route unhoused or post-acute patients to community-based organizations like Project Vision Hawaii4.
- [ ] **Business Sponsor:** DOH Population Health / SHPDA.
- [ ] **Data/Operational Owner:** DOH / Med-QUEST / Participating CBOs.
- [ ] **Required Data Sources:** EHR admission/discharge/transfer (ADT) feeds via HHIE, SDoH assessments, CBO capacity and bed-availability data.
- [ ] **Governance Risk:** High. Requires stringent HIPAA/MARS-E compliance, complex data-sharing agreements between competitive health systems, and direct political navigation against the incumbent Unite Us platform.
- [ ] **Measurable Outcome:** Statistically significant reduction in 30-day preventable emergency department readmission rates for unhoused or rural patients.

### 4. APCD Data Governance & Metadata Workflow Portal

- [ ] **The Opportunity:** The IV&V reports explicitly document severe struggles with data governance, business glossary approvals, and cross-vendor stakeholder management for the IDAP project9. A portal designed to manage metadata, route data access requests for approval, and digitize data steward workflows could immediately unblock the stalled DDI vendor.
- [ ] **Business Sponsor:** Ranjani Starr (HAP Administrator, Med-QUEST).
- [ ] **Data/Operational Owner:** HAP PMO / Data Governance Vendor.
- [ ] **Required Data Sources:** Data dictionaries from University of Hawaii, system access logs, Optum/Snowflake metadata feeds.
- [ ] **Governance Risk:** Medium. Directly addresses the state's most acute governance pain, but requires navigating a highly complex, politically charged, and defensive multi-vendor environment.
- [ ] **Measurable Outcome:** Reduction in data definition approval cycle times; accelerated timeline for IDAP user-acceptance testing (UAT).

## Top 10 Discovery Questions

To uncover actionable pain points without prematurely leading with technical features, the discovery phase must utilize a *Symptom → Operational Consequence → Business Impact* methodology.

**RHTP Grants & Program Execution**

- [ ] *(Symptom)* With Year 1 of the RHTP designated strictly as a pre-implementation phase, what mechanisms are SHPDA and DOH currently using to capture, score, and evaluate the influx of applications from rural hospitals and FQHCs for the competitive RHIN and RVBI funds?
- [ ] *(Consequence)* If sub-grantees—such as CBOs operating mobile care units—are forced to rely on manual or spreadsheet-based reporting, how does that fragment DOH’s ability to aggregate programmatic outcomes and accurately track fund utilization?
- [ ] *(Impact)* How would a unified, real-time view of sub-grantee performance and fund burn-rates affect Dr. Lewin's ability to prove AHEAD-model readiness and secure subsequent tranches of the $189M CMS cooperative agreement?

**Provider Network & Value-Based Readiness (RVBI)** 4. *(Symptom)* As SHPDA stands up the Virtual CIN/MSO to help independent providers manage downside risk, what systems are currently in place to track which practices have completed their necessary practice transformation coaching, EHR upgrades, and credentialing? 5. *(Consequence)* When independent providers lack transparent visibility into their own performance metrics and progression against AHEAD global budget targets, how does that impact their willingness to abandon fee-for-service and adopt these new care models? 6. *(Impact)* If the state cannot effectively support and track independent clinics as they attempt to absorb financial risk, how will that failure impact the overarching executive mandate to increase primary care capacity across the neighbor islands?

**Care Coordination & Interoperability (RHIN / Pili Ola)** 7. *(Symptom)* The RHTP explicitly calls for a new Community Care Coordination Hub (C-Hub). Operationally, how is the state currently routing transition-of-care alerts from HHIE to community partners operating the new medical respite centers? 8. *(Consequence)* With survey data indicating that 64% of providers still rely on manual data entry, how is the lack of seamless clinical data exchange delaying care interventions for unhoused or complex-need patients discharging to rural areas? 9. *(Impact)* What is the financial impact of preventable emergency department readmissions on the state's Medicaid budget if the Rural Respite Network cannot effectively coordinate wrap-around social services upon discharge?

**Data Governance (APCD / IDAP)** 10. *(Symptom/Impact)* Public IV&V reports indicate that siloed data governance and delayed clinical definitions have severely extended the APCD rollout. How is the HAP steering committee ensuring that the massive influx of new RHTP data streams (e.g., telehealth utilization, mobile care encounters) won't further exacerbate these data quality and integration bottlenecks?

## Evidence Gaps and Validation Plan

While the public record provides a robust strategic framework and exposes severe operational friction, several critical unknowns must be systematically validated during the initial discovery conversations to refine the solution architecture.

First, the internal system footprint for grants administration remains unverified. It is unknown whether SHPDA and DOH intend to force RHTP grant management through the state's central financial ERP (Infor), or if they possess the autonomy and budget to procure a modern, purpose-built Grants Management System (GMS). The validation plan requires asking SHPDA operations leaders how they currently distinguish the management workflows of federal block grants versus competitive state-allocated funds.

Second, the entrenchment of incumbent platforms in the social care space requires clarification. While Unite Us is utilized by the state Workforce Development Council, its penetration into DOH for the specific RHTP C-Hub initiative is unconfirmed. Discovery must ascertain whether DOH intends to expand existing social-referral contracts across departments or issue a net-new RFP for the RHIN Care Coordination Hub.

Third, the identity and operational maturity of the MSO Vendor must be uncovered. SHPDA planned to release an RFP for the CIN/MSO vendor in mid-2026. Determining if this vendor has been selected is critical, as they will act as the primary operational owner—and potential buyer—of the provider relationship management architecture.

Finally, Pacific Point's specific alignment and influence within DOH must be mapped. As a dominant local systems integrator, their footprint at DCCA and the Dept of Agriculture is well documented, but their relationship with DOH leadership regarding the RHTP is unknown. The validation plan involves engaging local channel alliances to determine if Pacific Point is already positioning for, or assisting in drafting the requirements for, the impending RHTP IT procurements.
